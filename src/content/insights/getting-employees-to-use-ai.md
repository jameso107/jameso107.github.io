Take a 260-person RV components maker in northern Indiana (an illustration, not a client): about 45 people in the office, 215 on two shifts in lamination, cabinetry and assembly. In the spring it bought 40 Copilot and ChatGPT seats. Use fell off after the first month, and by late summer three people were using them on real work. Second shift has decided AI means layoffs, the quality lead and both schedulers are a few years from retirement, and the owner wants to know what the seats bought.

Nobody sabotaged anything. The new way was never made the easy way, and nobody said what it was for. A company this size has no change management department; the owner and four or five managers are it. What keeps the seats idle is mostly seven things a sensible owner believes.

## Handing out licenses is the most common plan, and the weakest

*"We gave people access. The ones who want it will figure it out."*

In the US Chamber of Commerce's [2026 small business report](https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/Empowering-Small-Business-The-Impact-of-Technology-on-U.S.-Small-Business-Small-Business-Report-2026.pdf), an online survey of owners, 48% of small businesses using AI said they got workers ready by "providing AI tools and hoping employees learn to use them," up from 41% in 2025. No answer was more common, though on-the-job training was a point behind (owners could pick both). Most people are too busy to work out alone where a login belongs in their day: inventing a new way to do your job is a second job nobody assigned.

Introduce AI as a change to one task instead. "Here's Copilot" gets a shrug. "From now on, this is how we answer a dealer's lead-time question" changes what people do on Tuesday. Put the new way where the work already happens, in the shared inbox or beside the ERP screen, and retire the old reply template so the old path is the one that needs a reason. Otherwise people take the familiar one the first busy afternoon. [Pilot it with the people who'll use it](/insights/prototype-before-you-commit/) before it goes company-wide. One task per role fits on half a page:

| Role | The task | Their real document | Who they ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inside sales | Answer a dealer's lead-time question | Open orders, the dealer's email | Senior inside sales rep |
| Purchasing | Draft the daily expedite list | Open PO report from the ERP | Purchasing manager |
| Quality | Draft a complaint response | The nonconformance report | Quality lead |
| Scheduling | Summarize OEM build schedule changes | This week's and last week's releases | Senior scheduler |
| Lamination supervisor | Write the shift handoff note | Shift log, downtime sheet | Other shift's supervisor |
| Cabinetry lead | Find an answer in the work instructions | Work instructions, the print | Cabinetry lead, other shift |

## Your people are already using it, on their own accounts

*"Nobody here uses AI yet. We're starting from zero."*

You probably aren't. In the Census Bureau's [AI supplement](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html), surveyed over the winter of 2025 to 2026, 36% of the firms where workers had used AI for their jobs showed no sign the business itself had adopted it. Assume some of it is in your building, on personal accounts, with an OEM's print or your price list pasted in.

Before you write rules for it, send an anonymous survey with two questions:

1. Did you use any AI tool for work in the last month? Which one, for what, and on a work or personal account?
2. What's one task you'd hand off if you could?

Add an optional name line for anyone willing to show a coworker what they do with it. Use a Forms link for the office and paper slips in a locked box at the time clock, collected by HR rather than supervisors, or nobody will believe it's anonymous.

The first answer shows how much is going on, and in which tools. The second fills in the table above in your people's words. Build the first sessions around whoever left a name, since they've found uses you haven't. Move them onto company accounts, give everyone the one page on [what may go into which tool](/insights/ai-governance-for-owner-led-companies/), and don't punish anything the survey turns up, or the next one comes back blank.

## Plan on at least five hours of training a person

*"We did the training. The vendor ran a 45-minute session when the seats went live."*

BCG's [AI at Work 2025 survey](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/ai-at-work-momentum-builds-but-gaps-remain) of 10,635 office workers, mostly at large companies, found that 18% of those with no training were regular AI users, against 63% with 1 to 5 hours, 82% with 5 to 10 and 89% with more than 10. In-person training went with 12 points more regular use, and a coach with 14. It's an association (keener people may have sought more training), and [BCG's slide](https://web-assets.bcg.com/fd/0d/bcc5dfae4cbaa08c718b95b16cf5/ai-at-work-2025-slideshow-june-2025-edit-02.pdf) draws the line at "at least five hours of instruction." A 45-minute kickoff doesn't even make the 1 to 5 hour group.

We'd plan two sessions of about two and a half hours, a week apart, each person at a screen with their own documents. Session one: the one-page rules, what the freed time is for and the answer on jobs, from you (20 minutes); the first task on a live file (an hour); checking answers against the source (30 minutes); practice (40 minutes). In between, the person to ask sits beside each trainee once while they do the task for real. Session two fixes what broke, works the hard cases and sets up the weekly count.

Whoever the survey shows doing that role's task with AI should teach it, with prep time on the schedule. A reseller's feature tour is the 45-minute kickoff again. Being the person to ask afterward is a different job, and in our view it shouldn't go to your keenest user, because coworkers expect them to like it. Pick a respected skeptic: when a busy skeptic uses it, people take that as proof it saves time.

Five hours for each of the 40 seat holders is 200 hours. A $25 hourly wage, loaded by 1.43 for benefits and paid time off ([BLS, June 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm); a national average, so use your own payroll), is about $36 an hour, or roughly $7,200 of people's time. Set that beside your annual bill for the 40 seats.

State funders won't pay for a tool tour either. Michigan's [Going PRO guide for fiscal 2026](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/-/media/Project/Websites/leo/Documents/WD/Industry_Businesses/Talent-Fund/Employer-Guides/FY26-Going-PRO-Talent-Fund-Employer-Guide.pdf) lists "AI literacy training showcasing generic uses of common AI tools" as not eligible. Indiana's Employer Training Grant covers current staff, but [its FAQ](https://www.in.gov/dwd/nextleveljobs/employer/faqs) asks for more than 40 hours tied to a high-demand job and a 3% raise on completion. Two afternoons on Copilot won't qualify; an AI module in a longer credentialed program for a maintenance tech might. Check the state's page before counting on it.

## The floor is using it too

*"AI is for the office. Our people build cabinets."*

About a third of people in production jobs told the Real-Time Population Survey, which the St. Louis Fed [publishes on FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/RPSGENAIUSAGESHAREOCC21), that they use generative AI for their work (a four-quarter average through September 2026; single quarters have swung by more than nine points). An office-only rollout leaves them out, and what they use now is probably on their own phones.

Many floor people have no company login or phone at the station, so start with a shared terminal at the cell or the supervisor's laptop on a company account, and check your licenses before promising anyone a login. Give each area one use, taught by its own supervisor in the first 15 minutes of a shift and repeated for a few shifts. The five-hour figure came from office staff. Teach second shift on second shift. A recording of first shift's session tells them they're an afterthought, which is the rumor's favorite evidence. Three uses that fit this plant:

- Finding an answer in the work instructions: a cure time, a torque value, which hinge goes on which cabinet.
- Drafting the shift handoff note from the shift log and downtime sheet, so the next supervisor gets the same facts in the same order.
- Searching the maintenance log for what fixed the press the last three times it faulted the same way.

The tool finds the page and the person reads the value: ask it which page has the cure time, then read the number yourself. The instructions and the log have to be somewhere the tool can read, often after some scanning.

## It may help your newest people most

The instinct is to start with your best people. The evidence points the other way. In the [Quarterly Journal of Economics in May 2025](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond studied a support operation of 5,172 agents where an AI assistant was rolled out in stages. Issues resolved per hour rose 15% on average and about 30% for less experienced and lower-skilled agents, while the most experienced gained a little speed and lost a little quality. That was a software support desk, mostly in the Philippines, so the percentages won't transfer to a plant. The mechanism might: the authors found signs that the assistant passed what the best agents did on to the newer ones.

In a plant, the tool is likely to help the second-year scheduler more than the 25-year one, and to carry some of what the 25-year one knows, if they teach it. So make the retiring expert the reviewer. For the first month, give the senior scheduler an hour a week off other work to check the tool's drafts for the junior scheduler and note each correction with the reason: the customer whose Friday release always changes by Monday, the two jobs the line can't run back to back. Kept in one shared document, those notes become the tool's reference file. The expert keeps the final word, and more of what they know stays after they leave. Expect them to ask whether they're writing their replacement's manual. Answer it in the jobs talk.

## Silence about jobs reads as bad news

Second shift is already worried, and if you say nothing, the rumor fills the gap. In our experience, someone who says the tool isn't any good is usually worried about being replaced, or about being blamed when it gets something wrong.

Only 2% of firms using AI told the Census Bureau's [AI supplement](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html) that it had reduced their total employment over the previous six months, though firms using it in more parts of the business were likelier to report cuts. Where headcount does change, the Fed's [October 2026 Beige Book](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/beigebook202610-summary.htm) names the usual route: five Districts reported firms that "reduced staffing levels (through attrition), delayed hiring, or anticipated less job creation" after AI gains. Those are anecdotes. At a stable company, we'd expect that to mean a seat not refilled when someone retires. With three retirements coming, your people can do that arithmetic, so say it first.

A script to say in person on both shifts, from whoever people believe about jobs (usually the owner):

"We bought these tools to take retyping and hunting for answers off your plate. The time they free goes to [one named thing: dealer quotes back in two days instead of four, or no more Saturday overtime in cabinetry]. Whatever goes out under your name is yours, whether AI drafted it or not, so checking its work is part of the job. Nobody gets in trouble for catching its mistakes, and if one gets past a real check, we fix the check, like any other process miss. Nobody is losing their job because of this. When people retire from jobs these tools touch, we'll decide whether to refill those seats, and you'll hear it from me first."

Say "Nobody is losing their job" only if it's true. If a seat won't be refilled, say which role, when, and what the person in it does until then. Talk about roles, never names: retirement plans aren't yours to announce.

## Logins tell you nothing about whether it's working

*"The usage report says most of the seats were active last month."*

Microsoft's [Copilot usage report](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/admin/activity-reports/microsoft-365-copilot-usage) counts someone as active once they take a deliberate action, such as submitting a prompt, which says nothing about whether work moved. In BCG's [2026 AI at Work survey](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/ai-at-work-why-strategy-matters-more-than-tools), 66% of frontline regular AI users at companies with $100 million or more in revenue got limited or no guidance on what to do with the time they save. If nobody names where the hours go, they go into slightly faster email.

Decide where they go before anyone's trained, which for these 40 seats means now, and write it down: dealer quotes back in two days, the expedite list cleared before noon. Then each week, count one number per role, the share of that task done the new way. Of 60 dealer lead-time questions answered this week, how many started with the tool? An Outlook category on replies in the shared mailbox, or a "drafted with AI: yes or no" line on the handoff note, gets the count without a diary. Watch each role climb from having access to having tried it, using it weekly, and doing the task that way by default. Beside that number, [measure what the freed hours became](/insights/measuring-ai-roi/), the figure you'll want at renewal.

To decide who keeps a seat, the same Copilot report shows each person's last activity in each app over up to 180 days, with names hidden until an admin chooses to show them, so tell people before you look. OpenAI lists user analytics only under its Enterprise and Edu plans. For ChatGPT Business seats, the survey is your report. Our rule of thumb: a seat idle for 30 days goes to someone the survey found using AI on work that lives in their own mail and files. For a target, the median manufacturer using AI in the New York Fed's [2026 regional survey](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/09/businesses-are-using-ai-to-transform-work-not-cut-jobs/) had 7% of its workers using it: about 18 people at 260, against today's three.

Use the tool on your own work where people can see it, and in the weekly operations meeting, say how you used it that week. Then ask for the weekly number until the answer gets boring.

## Start with the survey

None of this needs a consultant to begin, and you may never need one. Outside help earns its fee when the task list outruns what your managers can teach, or a task needs a tool that reads your ERP, work instructions or maintenance log directly. That kind of tool can become routine: a custom LLM suite we built for a West Michigan automotive company is used daily by more than 400 employees.

On Monday, send the two-question survey, and read the answers on Friday. Fill in the task table from them, one task per role, office and floor. Then book the two sessions for the first group, and have the jobs conversation on both shifts before the first one.
