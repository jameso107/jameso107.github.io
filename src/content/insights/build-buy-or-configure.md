Take a 95-person civil engineering and surveying firm in Omaha, an illustration rather than a client. In one ordinary quarter, three things land on the managing principal's desk. A vendor wants a three-year contract for an AI platform that drafts proposals. A second-year project engineer has used AI coding tools to write a weekend script that files survey data and drafts field reports, and three crews now depend on it. And the Microsoft 365 renewal comes with an offer to add Copilot for all 95 people.

One looks like a purchase, one is already a build, and one is an upsell on something the firm owns. Four questions sort all three, along with the project accounting system the firm plans to replace next year. They also turn up the one tool worth building, which nobody has asked for.

## Start with what's already on the invoice

Configuring is the option nobody sells you. The vendor sells the product and the developer sells the build, but there's little money in telling you to switch on what you already pay for: the accounting module that never got set up, the automation tool in your Microsoft plan, the AI assistant that now ships with your seats.

As of fall 2026, Microsoft's [pricing page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/pricing/business) says Copilot Chat is included at no additional cost with eligible Microsoft 365 plans. Microsoft's [plan overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/plan/copilot-overview) explains the catch: Chat works from the web and from what someone pastes or has open, but can't search the company's mail and files on its own. The paid Copilot Business add-on can, at $21 per user per month on annual terms or $25.20 month to month ($30 for the enterprise version on enterprise plans). Google's [Workspace business plans](https://workspace.google.com/pricing) include Gemini too, though Business Starter leaves it out of Docs, Sheets and Drive. In NFIB's [2025 technology survey](https://www.nfib.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2025-NFIB-Technology-Survey.pdf), 44% of small employers using AI said it came inside products they already used.

Price the upsell before anyone signs. All 95 seats would be 95 x $21 x 12 = $23,940 a year at list. A crew chief turning field notes into a client update can paste them into the free Copilot Chat and needs no seat. The add-on is for the dozen people who write proposals and reports from the firm's own files: 12 x $21 x 12 = $3,024 a year. Microsoft's promotional first-year price of $18, for annual commitments bought by December 31, 2026 on a Business plan, makes this the quarter to settle the seat count.

## Four questions, in this order

Ask them one process at a time. Deciding once for the whole company ("we're a Microsoft shop") is how a firm ends up wrong about half its processes.

1. **Do customers choose you partly because of how you do this?** For an engineering firm, timesheets, invoicing and payroll: no. How a plan set gets checked before a city reviewer sees it: maybe. If it's no, don't build. You're choosing between configuring and buying.
2. **Do you already own pieces that do it?** Check before anyone sits through a demo. If what you pay for covers most of the job, configure, and keep a new product as the fallback.
3. **Does a product handle most of it the way you work now?** "Most" means you'd change a few steps, and none of the ones customers notice. Where customers don't care how it's done, change your steps to fit the product.
4. **Who keeps it running when the person who set it up leaves?** A product has its vendor. A configured chain or a custom build has only you, and an unowned chain breaks quietly: a connector changes, a report stops arriving, and a month passes before anyone asks where it went. If you can't name anyone, take the option that needs the least keeping, usually the product.

Building needs a yes on question 1, a no on 2 and 3, and a name for question 4.

The order matters because of where people start. Start from "we have someone who could build that" and you'll build what you should have bought. Start from a vendor's demo and you'll buy a product for the one process that's yours, then fight it for years.

If question 4 keeps coming up empty, you need an owner for software decisions and their upkeep: your IT provider, committed in writing, or a [fractional technology lead](/insights/fractional-technology-partner/).

BCG's [September 2026 advice to companies with $500 million to $5 billion in revenue](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/midmarket-firms-get-value-from-ai) draws the same line, buying "where they can capture value quickly" and building where proprietary data or expertise gives an edge. Putting configure first is our addition.

The project accounting replacement takes two questions. Clients don't hire an engineering firm for its invoicing, so it's configure or buy, and question 2 comes before any demo: which modules do you already license, and what does the vendor's newer edition add?

## Building got cheaper. Keeping it running didn't.

In McKinsey's [State of AI 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) survey, 32% of respondents said their organization had decided against buying at least one software product or feature because it could be built internally with agentic coding tools. More than a third of them work at companies with over $1 billion in revenue, where "built internally" means a software team. At 95 people, it means the engineer with the weekend script.

Her script works, and she found a need that wasn't on anyone's list. It also runs from her laptop, lives in her personal GitHub account, calls an AI model with an API key billed to her own credit card, and is documented only in her head. When the survey software changes its export format, only she can fix it.

Before anything homegrown becomes indispensable, put it through three checks:

- A named person can change it, and has hours set aside to do so.
- It's written down well enough for someone else to pick up: what it does, where it runs, what it connects to and how to switch it off.
- The code, logins, API keys and settings are in the company's name, not on a personal account or card.

Three yeses and it's a company tool. Anything less and it's a company process that walks out the door with one person.

## What the firm should do about each

### Configure Copilot for proposals, and make the platform beat it

Clients hire the firm for its engineers and its record with the city and county, so question 1 is a no. A proposal's resumes, project sheets and fee schedule are assembly. The project understanding and approach, the part the PMs say wins the shortlist, stays theirs to write. Question 2 settles it. Ten years of proposals sit in SharePoint, and the Copilot add-on can draft from them. The platform would probably pass question 3, which is why the demo looked good.

So configure. The only purchase is a new line on the Microsoft invoice. First, have the managed IT provider fix who can open what in SharePoint, and if it also resells your Microsoft licenses, get that work quoted separately from the seats. Microsoft [says](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/plan/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) Copilot surfaces company data a person has at least view permission for, so the partners' buy-sell agreement, saved years ago where everyone can open it, can turn up in an answer. Our [AI governance guide](/insights/ai-governance-for-owner-led-companies/) covers that cleanup and the vendor data terms. Then buy seats for the proposal writers and have the marketing coordinator build and own a clean library of project sheets and resumes. At 90 days, the Copilot usage report in the Microsoft 365 admin center shows who stopped using their seat; move those seats.

Before you answer the vendor, test both on the same work: two recent RFPs, one won and one lost, drafted by a proposal writer with Copilot and by the vendor in a trial on the same files. Compare the hours and how much the PM rewrote. If the platform wins by enough to cover its fee, sign for one year, with the renewal increase capped and your documents exportable in formats you can open without the vendor.

### The weekend script gets 30 days to pass three checks

By question 1, this should have been bought or configured, since clients don't pick the firm for how it files survey data. But three crews depend on it. If the engineer wants to own it, give her four hours a month on paper, move the code and API key into company accounts, and have her write down how it works, all within 30 days. Then settle what the checks miss:

- She wrote it on her own laptop over a weekend, so get the code assigned to the firm in writing, with your attorney's review, and consider paying her for it.
- The crews' field data has gone to an AI provider under her personal account's terms. Move it to a company account on business terms.
- Name the person who reads each drafted field report before it goes into the project file or to a client.
- The model it calls will be retired on its provider's schedule: Anthropic [promises](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations) at least 60 days' notice, and OpenAI's [most recent notice](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations), in October 2026, gave three months. Swapping the model and retesting is part of her four hours.

That upkeep is cheap. [BLS's June 2026 employer cost figures](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm) put benefits other than paid leave at about 29% on top of base pay, so an engineer on $80,000 costs about $103,200 a year, or about $55 an hour over the roughly 1,880 hours a person actually works. Four hours a month is 48 hours a year, about $2,600. Those are national averages; use your own. If she's billable and fully booked, price the hours at her billing rate instead. It's a bigger number, and still worth paying.

If she doesn't want it, or it can't pass, look for a field reporting feature in the data collector or project management software you already license, or in a product you buy. Either way, thank her.

### Build the plan-set checker small, after a pilot

The plan-set checker isn't on anyone's desk yet, and it's the one worth building. The firm's best repeat clients are cities and counties whose reviewers send plan sets back for missing local design standards. A tool that flags deviations from a client's manual before submittal cuts those review cycles, and quick approvals are what those clients come back for.

That's a yes on question 1, and nothing the firm owns does it. Question 3 needs a real test, because AI plan-check tools for civil drawings are on the market, and some say they'll check against local standards you upload. In this example, the strongest one, run on past submittals, can't handle the county's manual together with the firm's checklist and years of review comments.

A first version can handle what the manual spells out in words: required general notes, standard details, title block items. Anything measured off the drawing, like slopes and clearances, is the part to test hardest before anyone promises it. Cost turns on how many manuals it has to know, whether it reads PDFs or CAD files, and the engineer hours it takes to write each checklist.

Build it small, with an outside partner. In interviews with 52 organizations for its [2025 State of AI in Business report](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf), MIT's NANDA initiative found tools bought from or built with a partner, and customized to the work, reached deployment about twice as often as internal builds (about 67% against 33%, self-reported). Building here means deciding what the tool reads and how its output gets checked. No one trains a model. Put it under written terms: code and accounts in the firm's name, documentation, a handover plan, the monthly upkeep priced before work starts, and who swaps and retests the model when it's retired. Name the engineer who owns the checklist.

Test it on 50 past submittals, starting with those that came back with comments, and [pilot it before you commit](/insights/prototype-before-you-commit/) with the pass line set in advance as two numbers: how many of the reviewer comments citing the manual it would have caught, and how many false flags per sheet your engineers will tolerate.

Anyone who builds software for a living, us included, leans toward building. Hold their advice to the four questions.

## Count the whole bill over three years

A quote shows year one. Put each option on one page, over three years:

| Line | Buy | Configure | Build |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seats or usage | Per seat, repriced at renewal | Often paid; extras by use | Model usage, often small |
| Setup | Vendor onboarding | Your staff's hours | The largest line |
| Connections | Connectors, often extra | Some connectors extra | Part of the build |
| Training | Vendor material | You write it | You write it |
| Upkeep, hours a month | Settings and users | Always someone's | Someone's, plus model swaps |
| Getting data out | Get the format in writing | Already yours | Yours, if the code is |
| Price change | Cap it in the order form | Add-on prices move | Model costs falling |

Fill in the configure column for the proposal decision. Seats: 12 x $21 x 36 months = $9,072 at list, or $8,640 with the first year at $18, if the team sticks to what the seat includes (Microsoft now bills some advanced features, such as Copilot Cowork, by usage). The library: say 60 hours to build and three hours a month to keep current, 168 hours over three years. At the marketing coordinator's $58,000 salary, loaded the same way ($58,000 x 1.29 / 1,880, about $40 an hour), that's about $6,700. The platform needs the same library, though, so the $6,700 goes in both columns. What decides it is the vendor's three-year fee plus onboarding against about $9,000 in seats.

Multi-year deals get sold as protection from price increases, but for AI the cost underneath is falling. Stanford's [2025 AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report) found the cost of running a system performing at GPT-3.5's level fell more than 280-fold between November 2022 and October 2024. How much of that reaches your price is the vendor's call, and a three-year lock at 2026 prices settles it in their favor. Annual terms are the sensible middle. Microsoft charges 20% more month to month ($25.20 against $21).

## When the pitch says "agent"

If the proposal vendor calls its product an agent, ask what it does without a person clicking, and what happens when it's wrong. In June 2025, Gartner [predicted](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027. It also warned about "agent washing," vendors relabeling assistants and chatbots as agents.

If it drafts and a person reviews, it's an assistant, so price and judge it as one. If it sends anything to a client on its own, find out who catches the mistake, and how soon.

## This week, list what you pay for and what it already does

Without hiring anyone, list every software subscription with who owns it, the seat count, the renewal date and the AI features the current plan already includes. The forgotten ones turn up in a year of company card statements, the AP vendor list and the Microsoft 365 admin center's billing page. Put each renewal on the calendar 90 days early, while you can still change seats or terms. Then ask whoever built anything homegrown (the script, the Access database a long-tenured office manager built, the spreadsheet with macros) for one page on what it does, where it runs and whose accounts it uses.

When the proposal vendor calls back, have the list in front of you and ask what their product does that your list doesn't already cover.
