Two claims about AI get repeated to owners of Midwest companies, usually by someone with something to sell. One is that nearly every business already uses it. The other is that companies like yours are hopelessly behind. The Census Bureau's own survey says neither is true. What it does show is more useful: nearly two in three companies that adopted AI changed nothing about how the work gets done.

## Companies your size are mostly near the start

Every two weeks the Census Bureau asks US employers whether they used AI in any business function since the last round. Its [release of October 22, 2026](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/Employment%20Size%20Class.xlsx), collected October 5 to 18, breaks the answers down by company size:

| Employees | Used AI | Didn't know |
|---|---|---|
| 20 to 49 | 26% | 12% |
| 50 to 99 | 29% | 16% |
| 100 to 249 | 35% | 17% |
| 250 or more | 44% | 22% |

The rate rises with size, and it's moving. Among companies with 100 to 249 employees it went from 24% in late November 2025 to 35% in October, and 41% of them expect to be using AI within six months. A 150-person company that hasn't started is still in the majority, though a shrinking one: about two in three of its peers either said no (47%) or couldn't say (17%).

Look at the right-hand column too. At one in six companies with 100 to 249 employees, whoever answered couldn't say whether the business uses AI, probably because some weren't sure what counts and nobody asked the department leads. Asking them is the cheapest fix in this article.

The size bands mix every industry, and industry matters at least as much as size. Across all company sizes in that release, 44% of professional, scientific and technical services firms reported using AI, against 23% of manufacturers, 21% of wholesalers and 16% of construction firms ([Census sector estimates](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/Sector.xlsx)).

So a tool and die shop near the manufacturing average isn't behind its industry. An engineering firm or accounting practice that hasn't started has far more peers ahead of it, though even there a slight majority still said no or didn't know.

The higher figures you'll see elsewhere count more loosely: the [US Chamber of Commerce's 2026 survey](https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/Empowering-Small-Business-The-Impact-of-Technology-on-U.S.-Small-Business-Small-Business-Report-2026.pdf) reached 66% for businesses under 250 employees by counting any AI tool an owner names, free chatbots included.

## Michigan started late and has caught up to the middle

Census's state figures leave out companies that operate in more than one state, which use AI at higher rates, so compare Michigan with other states, not with the national number. They don't break out company size, so the table above is still a Michigan company's best peer comparison.

Last winter Michigan was near the bottom. Pooling six rounds from November 17, 2025 to February 8, 2026, Census found 14.7% of Michigan businesses using AI, which by our count tied Michigan with New Jersey for 40th among the 50 states, DC and Puerto Rico ([Census AI supplement tables](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/AI_Supplement_Table_2026.xlsx)).

By this fall it was in the middle. On our average of Census's six biweekly [state estimates](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/State.xlsx) from late July to mid-October 2026, Michigan ranked 24th of the 47 places with a complete set, right at the median, and 22.8% of Michigan businesses reported using AI in the October 5 to 18 round. Minnesota ranked 10th, Illinois and Ohio sat in the middle third with Michigan, and Indiana, Iowa and Wisconsin were in the bottom third.

Part of the slow start comes down to industry mix. Like most of its neighbors, Michigan has proportionally more manufacturers and fewer professional, scientific and technical services firms than the country ([Census County Business Patterns, 2023](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cbp/data/datasets.html)), so it would rank lower even if every company in it matched its national peers. By our arithmetic, though, the mix accounts for less than a third of Michigan's winter gap, and since it hasn't changed, none of the climb.

## What a first AI project has to work around here

The knowledge sits with people close to retirement. In many family-owned companies here, the best estimator has been quoting for 30 years, only the quality manager knows which customer wants certs in which format, and little of it is written down. So some of the most valuable early AI work is capture: getting past quotes, prints, specs and corrective actions into a form a newer person can search.

The systems are old, and they're staying. JobBOSS, Global Shop, an Epicor install two versions behind, a QuickBooks file and a lot of Excel: nobody should replace those to make room for AI. Most good first projects work on the PDFs, emails and spreadsheets that pile up around the ERP and leave it alone, since integration is where projects get slow and expensive.

Then there's re-quoting. In the National Association of Manufacturers' [third-quarter 2026 survey](https://nam.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Q3_2026_Writeup_Final.pdf), a small one with 220 responses, 85% of manufacturers with 50 to 499 employees named rising raw material costs as a top challenge. Each increase sends estimators back through open quotes, which makes quick re-pricing a fair first target.

## Most adopters bought the tool and stopped there

The Census supplement also asked adopters what they had changed in the past six months in order to use AI. Nationally, 64% said they had changed nothing ([Census AI supplement tables](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/AI_Supplement_Table_2026.xlsx)). Among firms with 100 to 249 employees it was 54%, and among Michigan businesses 68%. The changes that did happen were modest: about a quarter of the 100 to 249 group trained staff, a fifth built new workflows, and 8% brought in a vendor or consultant to install or integrate it.

Where they use it is lopsided too. About one in five firms with 100 to 249 employees used AI in sales and marketing, but fewer than one in twenty in quality management and fewer than one in thirty in purchasing or production ([Census AI supplement tables](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/AI_Supplement_Table_2026.xlsx)). In a manufacturer or distributor that's nearly the reverse of where the hours go. Certs, PPAP submissions, corrective actions and purchase orders eat senior people's time, and almost nobody has put AI there yet.

BCG's [September 2026 report on midmarket companies](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/midmarket-firms-get-value-from-ai) offers a rule of thumb for why the tool alone disappoints: in BCG's experience, only about 10% of the financial impact of AI comes from the algorithms and 20% from data and the technology stack. The other 70% comes from changes to people, processes and workflows, meaning who does what differently on an ordinary workday. Buying Copilot licenses covers the first 30% at best. The workflow change is the rest, and it's the part most of your peers skipped.

In a quoting office, that change looks like this. Before: the RFQ arrives by email. The estimator prints the 60-page spec, highlights material, tolerances, finish, cert and PPAP requirements, retypes the part numbers into the Epicor quote worksheet, and finds the customer's nonstandard payment terms on page 31 the day the quote is due, if at all.

After: the package goes into one shared folder. An approved assistant runs the same written checklist on every package (material and grade, tolerances that drive the process, finish, cert and PPAP level, packaging, delivery and payment terms, anything that departs from your standard terms) and returns a one-page summary with a page number on every line. The estimator checks each line against the cited page, then prices. Inside sales gets the list of exceptions and sends it with the quote, and the quote template gains a block for them.

Count what changed: who reads first, a checklist written once, a check step, a handoff to inside sales and a new block on the template. Buying the tool was one line of that. An assistant we built for a West Michigan manufacturer works from the same material, turning prints, specs and past quotes into structured estimates and a list of risks, and its Sales Engineers each got five or more hours a week back for customer work. [Our guide to automating quotes](/insights/automating-quoting-in-manufacturing/) goes further.

The same redesign works outside a plant. At an electrical contractor, the checklist runs on every bid set (scope, retainage, liquidated damages, required submittals, flow-down clauses), the estimator checks each line against the cited page, and anything you won't accept goes into the proposal as an exclusion.

## A smaller company can move faster, if someone keeps score

BCG's report rests on a survey of 152 CEOs at companies with more than $500 million in annual revenue, and it defines the midmarket as $500 million to $5 billion, far larger than the companies most readers here run. One advantage it describes probably grows as companies get smaller, though: "fewer layers between decision and execution." In a 120-person company the owner and the plant manager can agree on a change in one conversation, with no steering committee.

The report also shows where these firms fall down: just 11% of the midmarket CEOs said they had clearly identified the financial impact of their AI initiatives. From what we see, smaller companies are worse at this. Nobody writes down how long a quote takes today, so six months later nobody can say whether the new tool helped.

People who have been with you for 15 years are wondering what AI means for their jobs, and the evidence so far is reassuring. In the Census supplement, only 2% of the firms using AI said it had reduced their headcount, and 44% used it to help employees with tasks they already do, against 10% that used it to take over an employee's task ([Census working paper, April 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html)). Say that out loud, and be honest that some jobs will change as the paperwork moves.

## The next 90 days

You don't need a consultant for any of this.

### This week

Ask each department lead three questions, and say up front that nobody gets in trouble for an honest answer this round:

1. Which AI tools are people here using for work, and for what?
2. On a company account or a personal one?
3. What goes into them: customer emails, prints, pricing, employee information?

Expect more than you'd guess. In the Census supplement, 39% of firms with 100 to 249 employees said employees had used AI for work in the previous six months, while 25% said the business itself had used it in the previous two weeks ([Census AI supplement tables](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/AI_Supplement_Table_2026.xlsx)). If a lead says "nobody," ask again in a week, one on one. In our experience the honest answer often includes personal ChatGPT accounts with customer prints pasted into them, and you can't set rules for use you haven't found.

Then pick one document-heavy job where a senior person is the bottleneck, and count the hours. In quoting, have the estimator log the next ten RFQs, one line each: date in, pages in the package, minutes reading and checking, minutes pricing, date out, and whether a problem turned up after the quote went out. Tell them first what the log is for (to find the reading a tool could take over, not to grade them) and show them the result before anyone else sees it.

Say the log shows your senior estimator spends 15 hours a week reading RFQ packages, specs and prints before pricing anything. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' [June 2026 employer cost survey](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm) gives a way to load the wage: benefits other than paid leave add about 29% to pay, and paid time off means about 1,880 hours actually worked a year rather than 2,080, so each hour worked costs about the base hourly rate x 1.43. An estimator paid $44 an hour costs about $63. That's 15 x $63 x 50 weeks, or $47,250 a year of reading, from the one person you can least afford to tie up. Redo it with your own payroll and benefit costs, since a national average is only a stand-in.

Count the misses separately: a spec problem caught after the PO arrives costs more than the reading did, and never shows up in the hours. The hours and the misses together are your baseline, the first thing any vendor or consultant should ask you for.

### This month

Write a one-page rule for what can go into AI tools and what can't: nothing under NDA in a free tool, no pricing in anything that keeps or trains on what you paste, no export-controlled drawings (ITAR or EAR) in any AI tool until whoever owns compliance has cleared that specific tool, and a named person to ask when in doubt.

Before customer documents go into any tool, check that four things are true. People sign in with company accounts tied to your Microsoft or Google login, so access ends when someone leaves. The vendor's business terms say in writing that your inputs aren't used to train its models. An admin can see who has access. And someone has read your largest customers' NDA and export-control terms against it. If you're on Microsoft 365, find out what your plan already includes before buying anything. [AI governance for owner-led companies](/insights/ai-governance-for-owner-led-companies/) has more on vendor terms and data handling.

Train people on their own work. An hour of your CSRs running real customer emails through an approved tool, or your estimators running last month's RFQs through the checklist, beats a generic webinar and shows quickly where the tool helps and where it's confidently wrong.

### This quarter

Run one pilot on that one workflow, with the baseline, a target, a date to stop, and the controller signing off on the after-number. Filled in, it might read: about six weeks with two estimators and the checklist above, the first week on last month's packages with nobody relying on the output, four weeks on live RFQs with every line checked, and a week to decide. The target: reading time per package down by a share you set in advance, say half, with nothing missed in quote review. The stop rule: if by week four the summaries keep missing requirements, fix the checklist or drop the tool. [Prototype before you commit](/insights/prototype-before-you-commit/) covers kill criteria in more detail.

We've yet to see a 100-person company run five AI experiments at once and finish any of them well, so keep it to one. Walk away, too, from any proposal, ours included, that can't show a measured before-and-after for one workflow.

## Use free help first, and pay when someone has to build

For general questions, start with help that costs little or nothing. Michigan's [Small Business Development Center](https://michigansbdc.org/) offers one-on-one consulting at no cost, and its Growth Services team works with companies of 10 or more employees and $1 million or more in revenue. Manufacturers can call their state's Manufacturing Extension Partnership center. There's one in every state, and Michigan's is the [Michigan Manufacturing Technology Center](https://www.the-center.org/), which lists applied AI for manufacturing among its services. MEP help isn't free: centers are federally co-funded and charge client fees ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/mep/about-nist-mep)).

Paid help earns its fee once the work means building something on your own prints and quote history, or wiring a tool into the system you already run. Our piece on [when an AI consultant is worth hiring](/insights/what-an-ai-consultant-does/) covers how to vet one.

Whichever you call first, bring three things: the department leads' answers, the hours log and a draft of the one-page rule. With those on the table, an hour with an advisor is about your quoting desk instead of AI in general.
