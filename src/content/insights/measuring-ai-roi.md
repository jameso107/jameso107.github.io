Every fall, the AI subscriptions land on the same budget sheet as the copier lease. Take a 45-person independent insurance agency in the Twin Cities, mostly commercial lines (made up, to keep the numbers concrete). In the spring the owner bought 12 ChatGPT Business seats. The producers swear by them. The account managers are split. Now the vendor behind the agency management system (the AMS) is offering an AI add-on on a three-year term, and the 2027 budget is due before Thanksgiving.

The owner wants to know whether the seats paid for themselves and whether the add-on would. The office says the seats save a ton of time. What the owner needs is a number the agency's outside CPA could check line by line, and the seven lines below get there without new software.

## "It saves time" isn't a measurement yet

In McKinsey's [State of AI survey for 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai), 80% of 1,719 respondents said AI had improved their own productivity, and 37% said it had contributed to their organization's EBIT. More than a third of them work at companies with over $1 billion in revenue, but in our view the gap shows up at any size: when ten people each save an hour a week, the P&L doesn't change unless somebody decides what the hour is for.

The UK's Department for Business and Trade put a clock on it. Its [2025 evaluation](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68adbe409e1cebdd2c96a19d/dbt-microsoft-365-copilot-evaluation.pdf) of 1,000 Copilot licenses "did not find evidence that time savings have led to improved productivity." In a few tasks timed with and without the tool and scored blind by a second analyst, Copilot users summarized a report in about 13 minutes against 42, and did it better. An Excel analysis took them 25 minutes against 21 and came out worse, though users' own diaries said data analysis saved time. Just 11 people did the timed tasks, but the point stands: only the clock belongs in a budget.

## Line 1: one unit of work, counted from the system

Pick one unit of work that already leaves a record. At a commercial agency the natural one is a renewal: the account manager compares the carrier's renewal terms with the expiring policy (limits, deductibles, exclusions, endorsements, premium) and writes the summary the client sees.

Take the count from the expiration list in the AMS, over twelve months, because commercial renewals bunch up toward January 1. The agency's last twelve months show 840, about 70 a month.

Nobody timed anything before the seats arrived, which is fixable. Have the account managers who use the tool do every other renewal without it until you've timed ten ordinary ones and five messy ones (a multi-location account, a carrier's terms arriving ten days out). Timing the skeptics against the enthusiasts would only tell you who's faster. Keep the messy ones in their own column, because they're where a tool stumbles and easy files flatter it. Tell everyone the timing is for the tool's renewal and won't come up in anyone's review. For the agency, ordinary renewals average 80 minutes from opening the carrier's terms to sending the summary.

Two more numbers finish the baseline. Turnaround is how many days before expiration the summary reached the client, and you can still pull it for renewals before the seats arrived, because the AMS dated both ends. Send-backs are the summaries a senior account manager returns for a fix, and those you count from now on.

## Line 2: minutes saved, minus the checking

Measure the after column with two habits from the UK evaluation. Both fit in one week:

- A one-week diary. Each account manager logs every renewal they touch: file, ordinary or messy, tool or not, minutes.
- Five head-to-head tests on renewals already finished, so the right answer is known. One account manager redoes a file with the tool and another without, and they swap on the next. A senior colleague scores both summaries blind. Did each catch every change in limits, deductibles, exclusions, endorsements and premium? Would anything have been wrong in front of the client? Would they send it as is, after small edits, or redo it?

The tests take about 13 staff hours.

Then take out the checking before any minutes turn into dollars. Say an account manager now finishes the comparison in 40 minutes, then spends 10 more reading it against the policy forms. That reading isn't optional: a summary that misses a new exclusion is how an agency ends up on the phone with its E&O carrier. So the saving is 30 minutes a renewal, not 40, and about 10 on messy files. The same subtraction applies to [reading purchase orders and specs](/insights/ai-for-document-heavy-work/).

Count send-backs after, too. If they double, part of the saving has only moved to the senior account manager's desk.

## Line 3: hours into dollars, at a cost you can defend

The diary also shows how many renewals went through the tool, and only those get credit. Here it's three in four (630 of the 840), since some account managers still work the old way. If one in five of those is messy, the year's saving is 504 x 30 + 126 x 10 = 16,380 minutes, or 273 hours.

An account manager's hour costs more than the wage. In the Bureau of Labor Statistics' [June 2026 compensation release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm), private employers' total cost averaged $46.89 per hour worked against $32.82 in wages, about 1.43 times. So $26 an hour is about $37 loaded ($26 x 1.43 = $37.18).

From a salary the multiplier is smaller, because salary already pays for vacation and holidays, which BLS counts as a benefit: annual pay x 1.29, divided by the roughly 1,880 hours a person actually works. A $54,000 salary also comes to about $37. Adding paid time off on top counts it twice, and dividing by 2,080 paid hours instead of 1,880 worked understates the hour by a tenth.

Redo these national averages with your own payroll and benefit bills. In the same release, at establishments under 50 workers, wages are 74.1% of the cost, a multiplier nearer 1.35 (about $35 here), so $37 flatters the tool a little.

At $37 an hour, 273 hours is worth $10,101 a year. That's the value of the time. It isn't a return yet.

## Line 4: where the hours went

This line decides whether there's a return at all. The CPA will ask where the 23 or so hours a month went. The answers aren't worth the same:

- Overtime before the January 1 renewals shrinks, and payroll shows it. Hard savings.
- A retiring account manager isn't replaced, or a planned hire isn't made. Hard savings, the slowest to arrive and, at 45 people, the likeliest kind.
- Summaries reach clients 60 days before expiration instead of 30, which leaves time to remarket an account before it walks. Soft savings.
- Account managers prepare more new-business submissions for the producers. Soft savings too.

Soft savings are real, but they reach the P&L only if retention or new premium moves, so price them at the loaded rate as a stand-in and prove them with line 5. If you can't name a destination, this line is zero for now and the $10,101 is a ceiling. Left alone, freed time gets absorbed into faster email and lighter afternoons.

Decide where the hours go before you measure, and tell the people whose hours they are ([getting employees to use AI](/insights/getting-employees-to-use-ai/) covers that conversation). In [Bain's 2026 survey](https://www.bain.com/insights/your-ai-budget-is-growing-your-returns-arent-heres-why/) of 951 companies, 37% had targeted AI cost reductions of 11% to 20%, and nearly 40% of those that measured their outcomes landed at 0% to 10%. Bain doesn't say how big the companies were, and the lesson doesn't need it: you only see a gap like that if the target was written down first.

## Line 5: one number that should grow

Hours saved can't show whether the agency kept or wrote more business, so put one growth number beside them. In the [2026 Wharton and GBK study](https://ai.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Wharton-GBK-AI-Adoption-Report_Exec-Summary_FINAL.pdf) of US companies with 1,000 or more employees, 35% of departments that tracked at least one growth measure reported strongly positive returns from AI, against 13% of those tracking efficiency alone. It's a correlation, and the habit costs a 45-person agency nothing.

The number has to move fast enough to read at 90 days, and retention doesn't: at 70 renewals a month, one account won or lost moves the rate about 1.4 points. Pick a count the freed hours push up directly, such as renewals sent to at least one other carrier before expiration, or new-business submissions per producer per month. Report it as a count, and confirm it with retention on a rolling twelve months.

Turnaround belongs on this line too: it's the part of the work the client feels. A manufacturer's growth number might be the RFQs it used to turn away, measured the way [automated quoting](/insights/automating-quoting-in-manufacturing/) is.

## Line 6: everything it costs

The vendor's slide shows the license. The sheet needs the whole bill.

- Seats. As of fall 2026, OpenAI [lists ChatGPT Business](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing) at $20 per user per month billed annually, or $25 billed monthly. Twelve seats on annual billing: 12 x $20 x 12 = $2,880 a year. If they went on a card month to month, it's $3,600.
- Usage fees. Some add-ons charge by volume, so put this year's volume on the sheet and ask what next year's would cost.
- Your own people's time: writing the instructions, testing them on old files, training, fixing what broke in the first month. Call it 40 hours at $37, about $1,500. Nobody invoices you for it, but it's still a cost.
- The add-on at its full term: three years of fees, with any annual increase written in.

## Line 7: payback, held to your usual bar

Payback in months is the first-year cost divided by the monthly value of the hours that went somewhere line 4 can name. Its twin, break-even, holds up better when every saving is soft: the minutes per renewal the tool must save to pay for its first year. A tool that clears it easily is a safe renewal while the growth number is still out.

Borrow the bar from other technology. In [Deloitte's 2025 survey](https://www.deloitte.com/global/en/what-we-do/capabilities/ai/research/ai-roi-the-paradox-of-rising-investment-and-elusive-returns.html) of 1,854 senior executives in Europe and the Middle East, most said a typical AI use case took 2 to 4 years to deliver a satisfactory return, and only 6% reported payback inside a year. Deloitte sets that against the 7 to 12 months typically expected of technology investments, a benchmark it cites from an AWS report. Deloitte doesn't give company sizes.

Our view: a narrow first tool at a 45-person firm should clear the same 7 to 12 months you'd ask of a new phone system or an AMS module. If it can't, it's the wrong first tool or the wrong unit of measure. Write that bar down before the next tool starts, with the result that would make you [stop the pilot](/insights/prototype-before-you-commit/).

The arithmetic: first-year cost is $2,880 + $1,480 = $4,360. The time is worth $10,101 a year, about $842 a month, so payback is $4,360 / $842 = about 5.2 months (about 6 on monthly billing), comfortably inside the bar. Break-even is $4,360 / $37 = about 118 hours, or 7,070 minutes over 630 renewals: about 11 minutes each, against the 26 the tool saved on average.

For a spreadsheet, two formulas carry it:

- Hours saved = units done with the tool x minutes saved per unit, checking subtracted, / 60 (ordinary and messy separately, then added)
- Break-even minutes per unit = first-year cost / loaded hourly cost x 60 / units done with the tool

Read what's under that payback before it goes in the budget. It's all soft savings: the hours went into earlier renewals, and no payroll line shrank. If the remarketing count hasn't moved by the next reading, line 4 drops toward zero and the $10,101 is a ceiling again.

Charging all 12 seats to renewals is conservative. The producers' unit, a new-business submission from ACORD applications to cover note, deserves its own sheet.

Then the add-on. If it mostly does what the seats already do, its saving is the difference between the two tools, not another 30 minutes: two tools can't both claim the same minutes. Its break-even comes from the quote: three years of fees, divided by 36 and then by $37, is the hours a month it must free beyond the seats.

Ask how the vendor's hours-saved figure is built. Microsoft's [Copilot Dashboard](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/viva/insights/org-team-insights/copilot-dashboard), for one, credits 6 minutes to each search, summary or drafting action, from Microsoft's own 2023 studies, and prices the hours at a default $72, about twice this agency's loaded rate. Then run the add-on through these seven lines on your own past renewals. A vendor that won't allow that before a three-year term has told you something.

## Measure again at 90 days, then decide at renewal

A tool's first numbers are noisy: the enthusiasts use it on everything and the skeptics on nothing. Measure once use settles, at about 90 days for anything new, and again before every renewal. Put the next reading on the calendar the day you take this one.

At each reading, time a fresh ten ordinary and five messy renewals with the tool and without, recount the send-backs, and pull turnaround and the remarketing count. A tool that clears the bar stays. One that saves time but lets errors through usually needs tighter instructions or a stricter checking step. A seat nobody can name a weekly use for should go: $240 a year is small money, but it's still money spent on nothing. OpenAI's plan page lists user analytics as an Enterprise addition, so be ready to ask each seat holder what they used it for last week.

None of this needs a consultant. Once the timings are in, the sheet is an afternoon's work, and the agency's CPA is the right first reader. Outside help earns its fee in narrower cases: designing a test on your own past work before a multi-year add-on, untangling several tools that claim the same hours, or settling what counts as a saving when the owner and the CPA disagree.

This week, before anyone signs the add-on: pull the past twelve months of commercial renewals from the expiration list, and time ten ordinary renewals and five messy ones without the tool.
