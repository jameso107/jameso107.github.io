A typical case: the owner of a 320-person tier 2 auto supplier comes back from a customer's supplier day and asks the VP of operations, "Are we ready for AI?" It lands in the middle of price-downs and tariff re-quotes.

Asked about the whole company, the question gets two answers. The IT manager says the data's a mess. The quality engineer says she's used ChatGPT for months. Both are right, about different work. Asked about one workflow, it gets an answer you can act on Monday: 20 yes-or-no questions, answered by the people who do the work, tell you in an afternoon whether to pilot and what to fix first.

## Pick one workflow before you answer anything

A company whose sales process lives in three people's inboxes and whose accounts payable runs like a clock could put AI on payables next month and is nowhere near ready on sales. Score "the company" and you get an average that describes neither.

So write the workflow down in one line, start to finish: "customer engineering changes, from the customer's notice to the released drawing and updated control plan". "Quality" is a department, and departments score as mush. If you have several candidates, [rank them first](/insights/where-ai-pays-off-first/) by hours and payoff, then bring the top one here.

## Walk one real job through the building first

Questions 1 to 4 below invite optimism, because everyone describes the process as it's supposed to run. So first walk a real job with a notepad, which takes about thirty minutes:

1. Pick one that closed last week. A normal one, not last month's disaster.
2. Start where it arrived: the email, the customer portal, the EDI release.
3. Follow it desk to desk, noting the system, who touched it, what they typed and how long it sat.
4. Mark every retype and every point where someone had to ask a person because the documents didn't say.
5. Ask the people who handled it whether the next one would go the same way. If the answer is "depends who's working", question 2 is a no.

## The 20 questions

Count a yes only if you could show the evidence today: the procedure, the folder, the person's name. "Mostly" and "we're working on it" count as no. Answer with someone who does the work and one skeptic, and don't let the most enthusiastic person in the building hold the pen.

### The work

1. Can you name where the workflow starts and ends, and count how often it runs in a week? Yes means a number, from the ERP, the shared inbox or a week of tally marks.
2. Does everyone on this work do it the same way? Yes means two of them, asked separately, would list the same steps, in the same order, with the same checks.
3. Could a new hire learn it from what's written down? Yes means a current procedure or work instruction, not a binder nobody has opened since the last audit.
4. Are the exceptions known, and does each one go somewhere specific? Yes means everyone knows who gets the rush order, the missing print and the customer who calls instead of emailing.

If you're IATF 16949 or ISO 9001 certified, you probably start out more ready on these four than you think: procedures, document control and records already exist for much of the work, because an auditor asked for them.

### The data

5. Can you pull 50 past cases where you know the right answer? Yes means 50 finished examples, each with what came in and what went out. They're the test set you'll [run any tool against](/insights/ai-for-document-heavy-work/).
6. Do part numbers and customer names match across the ERP and the spreadsheets? Yes means 18 or more of 20 records in the spot check below agree everywhere.
7. Can someone find the documents? Yes means anyone could pull last March's examples in minutes. Scattered across a shared drive and a few inboxes still passes, since AI reads messy PDFs and email threads better than older software did. Paper in a filing cabinet doesn't.
8. Is the outcome recorded? Yes means you know which quotes won, which changes the customer accepted and which invoices were disputed.

If your data's a mess, you're in large company. Gartner [reported in February 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-02-26-lack-of-ai-ready-data-puts-ai-projects-at-risk) that 63% of organizations lacked the right data practices for AI or weren't sure they had them. It asked data management leaders, mostly at large organizations. At your size, "AI-ready" means what these four questions test, nothing grander.

Question 6's spot check measures the mess in an afternoon. Pull 20 recent cases and write down every number that names the part (at a molder: customer part number, internal number, revision level, mold number) wherever it appears: the ERP, document control, the control plan, the floor's spreadsheet. A record matches only if every place agrees. Our rule of thumb: 18 or more of 20 is a yes, 15 to 17 is a few weeks of cleanup, and under 15 makes a part-number cross-reference the first project.

### The people

9. Does one named person own this workflow, with hours freed for it? Yes means a name, and about four hours a week taken off that person's plate, agreed with their manager.
10. Do the people who do the work want it fixed? Yes means they've complained about it without being asked.
11. Has the owner or GM personally used an AI tool on a real email or report this month?
12. Will someone experienced check the output? Yes means a person who'd spot a wrong revision letter or resin grade on sight.

### The tools and access

13. Do people sign in to an approved AI tool with company accounts? Yes means business seats the company controls, and no work files in personal logins.
14. Is multi-factor sign-in on for everyone's email and company accounts, the owner's included?
15. Are file permissions clean? Yes means people can open only the folders they should. Microsoft [says Copilot](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/plan/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) shows people only company data they can already view, so an engineering folder everyone can open becomes an answer anyone can get.
16. Can the system holding the data export it or connect to other software? Yes means a scheduled report, a CSV export or an API your ERP vendor confirms in writing. If the work lives in email and PDFs, this one is usually a yes.

Question 13 matters because OpenAI's [pricing page](https://chatgpt.com/pricing/) says ChatGPT's Free and Plus plans train its models on what people put in unless they opt out, and its Business plan doesn't by default. Anyone already working in a personal account should say what went in, switch training off and move to a business seat. As of fall 2026, OpenAI [lists ChatGPT Business](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing) at $20 a user a month and Microsoft [lists Microsoft 365 Copilot Business](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/pricing/business) at $21, billed annually.

### The guardrails

17. Is there a written rule for what may go into AI tools? Yes means a one-page [AI rule](/insights/ai-governance-for-owner-led-companies/) that people have actually seen.
18. Has someone read your biggest customers' terms on sharing their prints and data? Yes means you know, customer by customer, whether their drawings may go into a cloud tool at all, and on what conditions.
19. Does a person check anything that leaves the building or changes a record? Yes means AI drafts and a person approves, so nothing reaches a customer or posts to the ERP unseen.
20. Can the business live with a visible, caught mistake in the first weeks? Yes means a wrong draft caught by a reviewer is an annoyance, not a customer escape.

Question 18 carries the most downside for a supplier. For your top three customers, pull the PO terms and conditions, any NDA and the customer-specific requirements or supplier quality manual, and search them for "confidential", "proprietary", "third party" and "disclose". Then write one line per customer: drawings may go into a business-account AI tool, go in on conditions, or not go in at all. If the wording is unclear, ask the buyer in writing, or your attorney.

## Reading your score

Check three questions before you count the yeses. A no on any of them stops a pilot whatever the total: a named owner with hours (question 9), past cases with known answers (question 5) and a person checking anything binding (question 19). In our experience, a workflow that scores 17 with one of those missing stalls within a couple of months. With all three in place, the total tells you what to do:

- 16 to 20: run a pilot. We'd plan on about six weeks, first on past cases where a wrong answer costs nothing, then on live work with a person approving everything, and [decide before you start what would make you stop](/insights/prototype-before-you-commit/).
- 11 to 15: pilot while you fix the weakest group. Most good first projects we see land here.
- 6 to 10: a few weeks of housekeeping first, then score it again.
- 5 or fewer: the work itself needs sorting out before any tool will help. Start with the table below.

## What a "no" costs to fix

Money is rarely what holds a company back. In the Census Bureau's [AI supplement spreadsheet](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/AI_Supplement_Table_2026.xlsx), collected from November 2025 to February 2026, only 8.5% of firms with 100 to 249 employees and no plans to use AI in the next six months said it was too expensive. More often they said AI didn't apply to them (47.9%), which for some is true. Many cited privacy or security worries (28.5%) or not knowing what AI can do (24.5%), and questions 11, 13 and 17 deal with both. Firms could give several reasons.

| Gap | Cheapest fix | Who | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| No named owner | Name one, free four hours a week | Owner or GM | One conversation |
| Steps differ by person | Two veterans write them, a third follows them for a week | Those doing the work | A few weeks |
| Past cases not findable | Save each finished case, inputs and outcome, in one place | Workflow owner | Weeks to months |
| Part numbers don't match | The spot check, then cleanup | IT and the item master's keeper | An afternoon to a project |
| Personal AI accounts only | Business seats for the people on this work | IT manager | About a week |
| No written rule | One page: approved tools, what never goes in, who checks | GM and the contracts person | An afternoon |
| No review step | An approval before anything goes out or posts | Workflow owner | A day |

Almost none of these needs a vendor, and a platform bought to fix a process problem gives you the same problem with a subscription attached.

## Two workflows at an Ohio supplier

Back to that supplier, a hypothetical one: injection molding and sub-assembly in northwest Ohio, IATF 16949 certified. The VP scores two workflows with the quality engineer, the production scheduler and the IT manager as the skeptic.

Customer engineering changes score 16. The work and data groups go eight for eight. Change control is a written procedure the registrar audits, and the walk matched it. Three years of changes sit in document control with the notice, revised print and disposition, and 19 of 20 spot-checked records agreed. For question 18, the quality manager pulled the three biggest tier 1s' terms: two allow drawings in a business-account tool, and one bars sending them to any third party, so that customer's changes stay out of the pilot.

Of the four nos, one is a veto: no workflow owner with hours, since the quality manager owns changes today and retires next year. The others are the company owner's own AI use, company accounts and a written rule. The quality engineer's ChatGPT is a personal login. She says it has seen her own reports, never a customer print, and switches training off that afternoon.

The pilot is narrow. When a change notice arrives, an assistant reads it with the old and new print revisions and drafts the impact list she now builds by hand: changed characteristics, the control plan lines, PFMEA lines and gauges they touch, any mold work, and whether the customer will want a PPAP resubmission. She approves or corrects every line. It runs first on 50 past changes, scored on whether it caught every changed characteristic (a missed dimension is the failure to watch for) and whether checking its list beat building one. If it goes live, the AI step and its approver get written into the change-control procedure.

Production scheduling scores 8, with two vetoes. The real plan lives in the scheduler's spreadsheet and his head: which presses run which molds, which color changes to avoid, which tier 1 phones in hot orders. His backup does it differently. Yesterday's schedule gets overwritten, so there are no 50 past cases (question 5), and the spreadsheet's press nicknames and shortened part numbers don't match the ERP. The scheduler has no hours to spare (question 9), and a bad schedule usually gets caught on the floor after someone has hung the wrong mold, so question 20 is a no too.

So the VP's answer to the owner fits in three lines:

- Engineering changes: 16 of 20. Make the quality engineer the owner (she wants it fixed) and free four hours of her week. Then test on 50 past changes and go live with every draft approved.
- Before the pilot: business seats for the five people on it ($1,200 to $1,260 a year at list), a one-page AI rule, and you trying an approved tool on your own email.
- Scheduling: not ready. The scheduler and his backup write down the rules they actually use and save each day's schedule with a note on what changed. Fifty working days is about ten weeks, so we score it again then.

That work pays even if AI never touches scheduling: the plant shouldn't run on one person's memory, and the quality manager retiring next year is the same risk with a date on it.

## When outside help is worth paying for

You don't need a consultant, us included, to answer 20 questions about your own business. Outside help earns its fee when you're choosing among several workflows with money attached, or when the people answering are too close to see clearly. An outside team interviews people you wouldn't think to ask (the second-shift lead, the CSR who takes the tier 1's calls) and marks a no where an insider would round up. That's our [AI Audit](/services/#audit).

Manufacturers can also call their state's Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) center, which is federally co-funded and charges fees. In Michigan, [The Center](https://www.the-center.org/Our-Services/Applied-AI-for-Manufacturing) lists AI for production scheduling and visual inspection. In Ohio, NIST [awarded the center](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/09/nist-awards-more-30-million-mep-centers-11-states-and-puerto-rico) to the Ohio Manufacturers' Association's institute in September 2026. The OMA's center hadn't published an AI offer when we looked in November 2026, so ask.

Either way, start this week: pick one workflow, walk one real job through the building, and answer the 20 with someone who does the work and the most skeptical person you can find. Then "are we ready for AI?" has an answer with a number on it.
