Take a 230-person, family-owned private-label food processor in the Chicago suburbs. Its biggest grocery customer's supplier questionnaire has a new line this year: describe how AI tools are used with our specifications and data. There's no CIO, so the question lands on the CFO, who also runs IT with an outside MSP and oversees HR. The HR coordinator wants an AI résumé screener for 40 seasonal hires. An R&D tech has been pasting customer specs into a personal ChatGPT account. The insurance renewal is coming, and the owner wants an AI policy by year end.

The questionnaire needs a true answer, and today nobody in the building could write one.

## Find out what's already happening before you write anything

You can't write rules for use you don't know about, and there's almost certainly some. In KPMG and the University of Melbourne's [2025 trust study](https://kpmg.com/us/en/media/news/trust-in-ai-2025.html), 44% of US workers surveyed said they use AI at work in ways their employer hasn't authorized, and 46% said they'd uploaded sensitive company information to public AI platforms. Most owners haven't written anything down either: in Goldman Sachs 10,000 Small Businesses' [October 2026 report](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/community-transformation/small-business-ai-institute/ai-impact-report.pdf), 74% of the program's alumni who use AI had no formal policy for employee use.

So before drafting, ask each department lead (R&D, quality, HR, customer service, the office) which AI tools their people use and on which accounts, notetakers on Teams calls included. Promise no penalties this round, so the next person with a pasted spec speaks up. [Getting employees to use AI](/insights/getting-employees-to-use-ai/) has a two-question anonymous survey for everyone else. The answers are your inventory, and the tools people already prefer are the ones to approve first.

## The one-page policy, clause by clause

Eight clauses, short enough to print on one page. The italic text is the clause: copy it, fill in the brackets, and keep the notes for yourself. Train everyone on it for an hour, with the owner in the room and your own documents as examples, then put it in the handbook and have everyone sign it, seasonal hires included. If year end is tight, do clauses 1 to 3 first, because they stop the pasting already happening.

### 1. One person owns these rules

*[Name], our [title], owns this policy, the approved AI tool list and our response when something goes wrong. It applies to everyone here, owners and family members included, and is reviewed every [month] and [month].*

Name a person, not a committee: usually the controller, the CFO or the operations lead, someone who already reads contracts and can say yes or no to a tool by Friday. We wouldn't give it to the MSP, which is a vendor itself. A committee won't take a 7 p.m. call about a spec that went somewhere it shouldn't.

### 2. Company work happens in company accounts on approved tools

*Our approved AI tools are [list], used with your company account. Never put company, customer or employee information that isn't already public into a personal AI account, on a computer or on your phone.*

This clause does the most work, because the account type decides what the vendor may do with what you paste. As of November 2026, OpenAI's [ChatGPT pricing page](https://chatgpt.com/pricing/) marks its Free, Go, Plus and Pro plans as training its models on your content unless you opt out, while [ChatGPT Business](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing) doesn't train on business data by default. Anthropic's [pricing page](https://claude.com/pricing) shows the same split between its individual Claude plans and Claude Team. So the R&D tech's customer specs went in under terms that allow training, unless someone found the setting.

You may already have a default tool for everyday internal work: Microsoft says [Copilot Chat](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/plan/copilot-overview) comes with eligible Microsoft 365 plans at no extra cost, can't search company files on its own, and keeps prompts under the same terms as your email when people sign in with a work account. For people who need more, OpenAI and Anthropic both list their business plans at $20 per user per month billed annually as of November 2026, with single sign-on: 25 seats x $20 x 12 = $6,000 a year.

Don't ban AI outright. A ban doesn't stop the R&D tech with a deadline. It moves the work to a personal phone, where you can't see it and the consumer terms apply.

### 3. Three kinds of information, and where each may go

*Green (public information and general questions with no company details): any AI tool. Yellow (internal information that isn't secret): approved tools on company accounts only. Red (customer specifications and formulas, anything under an NDA, pricing and costs, employee and payroll records, and anything a law or contract regulates): only tools [Name] has cleared for red, as far as the customer's contract allows. Export-controlled drawings and controlled unclassified information go into no AI tool until [export compliance owner] approves the setup.*

People have to sort a document in two seconds or they won't sort it. Five kinds is too many to remember, and two lumps the production schedule in with a customer's formula.

| | Food processor | Parts supplier | Accounting or insurance firm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green | Product sheets, a food safety question | Catalog, published specs | A newsletter draft, a tax code question |
| Yellow | SOPs, the production schedule | Work instructions, scrap reports | Engagement templates, internal procedures |
| Red | Customer specs and formulas, costed recipes, HR files | Customer prints, PPAP packages, quotes | Client financials, Social Security numbers, policy files |

Cleared for red means a business account that doesn't train on your data, a retention period someone has read, and ideally a connection to the system where the file lives. OpenAI and Anthropic list retention controls only on their enterprise plans, so on ChatGPT Business or Claude Team the vendor's default applies. For now, keep red out of chat tools and clear one named use at a time.

Read the customer's NDA or supply agreement too. Look for a use restriction ("solely for the purpose of"), limits on disclosure to third parties and a return-or-destroy clause. Some bar sharing with any third party, and an AI vendor is one.

Export-controlled drawings get their own approver because ITAR's [technical data](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/120.33) and the EAR's [technology](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/15/772.1) both include drawings, and that call isn't the CFO's.

### 4. A person checks anything that leaves the building or changes a record

*AI output is a draft. Before anything AI helped produce leaves the company (a quote, a certificate, a label, a letter, an answer to a customer) or changes a number in our systems, the person whose name goes on it checks it against the source and owns every number and statement in it.*

In 2023 a federal judge in New York fined two lawyers and their firm $5,000 for citing cases ChatGPT had made up, then standing behind them. The [sanctions order in Mata v. Avianca](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0_3.pdf) didn't blame the tool:

> [T]here is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance. But existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings.

Put your own titles where it says attorneys: the quality manager who signs a certificate of analysis, whoever approves a label's allergen statement, the estimator on a quote, the CFO who signs the questionnaire. How hard each one checks should depend on [what a mistake would cost](/insights/ai-for-document-heavy-work/).

### 5. AI in decisions about people gets notice and a human

*Any tool that screens, ranks or scores applicants or employees needs approval from [Name] and HR first. We tell people when one is used, keep zip-code filters off, and a person makes and can explain every decision. Anyone turned down can ask a person to look again, and we keep the notice, the tool's output and the reason for each decision for three years.*

Illinois is why this clause exists. Since January 1, 2026, an amendment to the Illinois Human Rights Act (Public Act 103-0804) has barred employers from using AI that has the effect of discriminating against protected classes in hiring and other employment decisions, barred using [zip codes as a proxy](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2025/02/illinois-takes-aim-artificial-intelligence-employment/) for a protected class, and required notice. The state's proposed notice rules were [withdrawn and postponed on June 2, 2026](https://natlawreview.com/article/illinois-postpones-proposed-regulations-ai-employment), but the statute applies without them. If a tool analyzes recorded video interviews, Illinois's 2020 [Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/en/insights/blogs/employment-law-observer/illinois-adopts-new-ai-in-employment-regulations-what-employers-need-to-know-for-2026.html) also requires notice, an explanation of how the AI works and consent first.

For the food processor, that means listing every tool that touches the 40 seasonal applicants (AI features in the job board and payroll suite included), turning off zip-code filters and any distance filter that works the same way, and asking counsel about the employee threshold and out-of-state applicants. We'd also ask whether 40 seasonal hires need ranking at all. If HR still wants it, get in writing what the tool ranks on, whether it can show why an applicant landed where they did, and who answers a discrimination claim.

With no state form yet, here's a notice for counsel to adapt for the posting, the application page and the handbook: "We use [tool], software that uses artificial intelligence, to [sort applications against this posting's requirements]. A person reviews every application and makes every hiring decision. To ask a question or request a human review, contact [name, email]."

Ohio and Indiana have no AI-specific employment statute, [Bricker Graydon](https://www.bricker.com/employment-law-report/ai-based-hiring-2026-developments-employers-cant-ignore) reports, and Michigan's [HB 5579](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Bills/Bill?ObjectName=2026-HB-5579) and [SB 1077](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Bills/Bill?ObjectName=2026-SB-1077) sit in committee. Federal discrimination law still applies in all three, and Bricker notes that a federal court let Mobley v. Workday, a suit against an AI screening vendor, go forward.

### 6. New tools get checked before data flows to them

*Before a new AI tool, or a new AI feature in software we already use, gets yellow or red information, [Name] gets written answers to five questions and adds it to the approved list:*

1. *Does it train on our data, and is that off by default on the plan we'd buy?*
2. *Where is our data stored, and for how long?*
3. *Can we delete it, and how?*
4. *Who at the vendor can see it?*
5. *What happens to our data when we leave?*

The ERP, the HR suite and the e-signature service you already pay for keep adding AI, so ask each vendor at renewal which AI features are on by default. An answer a rep can't point to in the terms or the data processing addendum counts as a no.

### 7. Fix who can open what before AI can search it

*Before an AI assistant is connected to our email or files, [Name] and our IT provider check who can open what and close anything open too wide. Every AI tool sits behind single sign-on with multi-factor authentication, so when someone leaves, their AI access ends with every other access.*

Microsoft [says](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/plan/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) Copilot shows a person only company data they already have permission to view, and Google [says the same](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/generative-ai/generative-ai-in-google-workspace-privacy-hub) of Gemini. That includes the payroll export someone shared with the whole company years ago, which stayed private only because nobody knew to look for it. Once an assistant can search everything a user can open, it's one question away.

Before switching one on, have the MSP check:

- Files, folders and sites open to everyone in the company (an all-staff group, a team site anyone can join)
- Who can open the HR, payroll and finance folders, and the owner's personal folders
- Accounts and drives that belonged to people who've left
- Links that let anyone open a file: Microsoft says they [don't put the file in Copilot's reach](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/shareable-links-anyone-specific-people-organization) on their own, but they work for whoever they're forwarded to

Get the MSP's findings and fixes in writing, then have an ordinary employee ask the assistant for salary information. It's a spot check, not proof.

### 8. When something goes wrong, tell the owner of these rules

*If red information goes into the wrong tool, if AI output causes a mistake that reaches a customer, an employee or the books, or if a vendor reports a breach, tell [Name] the same day. A mistake reported that day is handled as a fix, and nobody is disciplined for reporting it. Hiding one, or repeating it after being told, is handled like any other breach of confidentiality.*

Someone afraid of being fired deletes the chat, and you hear about it weeks later from the customer. So the promise has to be true, and seen to be kept.

To respond, write down what went where and when, delete the conversation or the share, ask the vendor what it keeps, and check the customer's contract for a duty to tell them. Then fix the default. If someone used a personal account because the approved tool couldn't do the job, get a better approved tool.

## Ask your broker three questions at renewal

Fenwick's [June 2026 review](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/end-silent-ai-emerging-ai-exclusions-coverage-fragmentation-and-practical-implications) reports that ISO introduced a generative AI exclusion for general liability policies in January 2026 and that several insurers have added broad AI exclusions to D&O and employment practices policies. Verisk, which files the ISO forms, lists its endorsements as [optional](https://core.verisk.com/LOB-PAGES/General-Liability-Hub), so yours may or may not carry one. Ask:

1. Does any of our policies (general liability, cyber, E&O, D&O, employment practices) carry an AI exclusion or sublimit?
2. How does each one define "AI"? A broad definition can reach the AI built into ordinary software.
3. What do this year's applications ask about our AI use?

Ask about the résumé screener under the employment practices policy, and send the broker your tool list, the AI inventory Fenwick recommends keeping.

## Before the questionnaire is due

Start with the paste you already know about, and treat it as the first report under clause 8. Have the tech turn off training in that account and delete those chats. Note whose specs went in, and if an affected customer's agreement says you must tell them, call counsel before anything goes back to the customer.

Then the answer can be short: "We use [Microsoft Copilot Chat and ChatGPT Business] with company accounts whose terms, as of [date], exclude training on our data. Your specifications and formulas are restricted under our written AI policy (attached) and go into no AI tool [Name] hasn't cleared for them in writing." If the questionnaire asks whether customer data has ever gone into a public AI tool, the true answer may be yes, plus what you did about it. That beats an answer the customer later learns was wrong.

You don't need a consultant to write this page. Outside help earns its fee when AI is about to connect to systems holding red information (the ERP, the HR suite, the shared drives), when a customer asks for controls you can't yet show, or when nobody inside has the hours to own it, which is what a [fractional technology partner](/insights/fractional-technology-partner/) is for.

This week, email the department leads the inventory question and ask the MSP for a list of everything open to the whole company, because that cleanup takes longest. By Friday, fill in the brackets and hand the page to the owner. When the grocery chain asks how AI is used with its data, the answer is two true sentences and an attachment.
