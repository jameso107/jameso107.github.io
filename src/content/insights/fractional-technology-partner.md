At a 180-person commercial furniture maker in West Michigan (an illustration, not a client), the shipping supervisor's Excel macro, the one that turns each day's orders into pick lists, breaks for the third month running. It's the smallest of five technology problems on the president's desk that week. The ERP vendor announces end of support for the on-premises version the company runs. The managed service provider (MSP) sends its January renewal, with a price increase and a new "AI readiness" add-on. The cyber insurance application now asks how the company uses AI. And a dealer portal vendor sends a quote.

None of those is a help desk ticket. Each is a decision with money and a date attached. The controller signs the software contracts because the invoices arrive there. The IT manager, self-taught and 20-some years in, keeps everything running but has never chosen an ERP. Deciding isn't anyone's job, so the president of this family-owned, second-generation company has become its chief technology officer by default.

Most of what's written about fractional CTOs assumes a software startup. Here the job is making decisions like these five, whatever you call the role: fractional CTO, part-time IT director or technology lead. At this size it usually takes about a day a week.

## Count the technology decisions you're making by default

Do this before you call anyone, us included. Ask the controller for 12 months of payments to software, IT and telecom vendors, plus the card statements and expense reports where AI subscriptions hide, and turn every open decision into a row. Then add the ones that never send an invoice:

| Decision | Who decides now | Deadline | Money a year |
|---|---|---|---|
| ERP: upgrade, cloud or replace | Nobody yet | End of support | Licenses, support, the move |
| MSP contract and add-ons | Controller | January | Monthly fee x 12 |
| Cyber insurance renewal | Controller and broker | Policy date | Premium |
| Microsoft 365 and Copilot | Whoever orders seats | Renewal date | Seats x price |
| Website or dealer portal | Sales manager | Dealer pressure | Quote plus upkeep |
| Backups and a restore test | The MSP, assumed | Never tested | Days without data |
| AI tools and rules | Each employee | Already happening | Seats, plus the risk |
| The spreadsheet one person keeps alive | That person | Next time it breaks | A day of shipping |

Rough numbers in the last column are fine. Blanks aren't. To fill in the backups row, ask the MSP to restore the ERP database from a date you pick onto a spare machine, and to tell you how long it took. Until someone has, that row is a guess.

The decisions depend on each other, too. A dealer portal earns its keep by reading inventory, pricing and order status straight from the ERP, so choosing it before the ERP decision means choosing it twice. In the meantime, give the dealers a date for the portal and a weekly ship-date report from the ERP.

## Two dates won't wait for anyone you hire

Read the MSP contract's notice clause first: if it renews automatically, the notice deadline can fall well before January. Ask to renew the current scope for three or six months, or month to month, without the AI readiness add-on, and ask what the add-on delivers, in sentences. If nobody can write that down, it isn't ready to buy. The real renegotiation waits until you have an inventory.

On the insurance application, answer what's true today. If it asks about a written AI policy and you don't have one, say so, and write [one-page rules](/insights/ai-governance-for-owner-led-companies/) this month. Coverage is shifting too. Verisk's ISO unit, which writes standard policy forms for insurers, has filed [optional](https://core.verisk.com/LOB-PAGES/General-Liability-Hub) generative AI exclusions for general liability. [Fenwick & West's June 2026 analysis](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/end-silent-ai-emerging-ai-exclusions-coverage-fragmentation-and-practical-implications) dates them to January 2026 and notes that some insurers have added broad AI exclusions to D&O and employment practices policies. Ask the broker which of your policies carry one and how each defines AI.

## Four kinds of outside help, and what each is paid to do

A company this size already pays for outside technology help from two or three directions, and the advice tends to follow the payment.

| Who | Paid for | Incentive points to | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSP | Devices, users, services | More seats and services | Keeping things running |
| Software vendor or reseller | Licenses and renewals | The next version | Knowing its own product |
| Project consultant or integrator | Delivering a project | The next project | A scoped job with an end |
| Fractional technology lead | Part of a leader's time | Staying hired | Deciding what to buy, keep or drop |

None of the first three is doing anything wrong, and a good MSP keeps patching and backups in order for years. It still shouldn't decide whether you need its AI readiness add-on, any more than the ERP reseller should decide whether you need the next version. Only the last row is paid to say "keep what you have," and only if it takes no money from the other three. Its own pull is toward staying hired, which is what the yearly review and the exit terms below are for.

## Fractional or full-time comes down to hours

Tally the executive-level work (decisions, contracts, risk, the roadmap and coaching) and leave out tickets and password resets. For the furniture maker's first year, a fair monthly estimate:

- A weekly working session with the IT manager and controller, with prep: 8 hours
- The monthly leadership meeting and the roadmap: 4 hours
- Vendors and contracts (renewals, negotiations, evaluations): 10 hours
- Risk (the MSP's security review, a restore test, insurance, AI rules): 6 hours
- Coaching the IT manager and writing things down: 6 hours

That's 34 hours a month, about a day a week. An ERP selection adds 15 to 25 hours a month while it runs, still under two days a week.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' [Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/management/computer-and-information-systems-managers.htm) puts the national median wage for computer and information systems managers at $175,140 in May 2025. Add about 29% for benefits and payroll taxes other than paid leave, which the salary already covers ([BLS employer cost data for June 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm)), and it's about $226,000 a year. Both are national figures across employers of every size, so redo the math with your own payroll.

A full-time hire works about 1,880 hours a year (2,080, less about five weeks of holidays, vacation and sick days). Against a tally of 408 hours (34 x 12), that's about $550 an hour of the work you need, or $18,800 a month. The other 1,400-plus hours go to help desk work the MSP already covers or to projects invented to fill the calendar. A fractional lead covering the same tally is cheaper as long as it costs less than that a month ([how such arrangements are priced](/insights/how-much-does-consulting-cost/)).

The fair objection: the hire could take some work back from the MSP. Cut, say, $3,000 a month from that contract (use your own figure) and the hire costs about $190,000, or $465 an hour of the tally. The gap narrows but stays wide, which is why we think a full-time technology executive is usually the wrong first answer at this size.

## What to settle before you sign

**Decision rights.** The lead decides security settings, admin access, standards for anything new, and vendor choices under a dollar limit (start at the controller's own approval limit). Above it, and on the roadmap and budget, the lead recommends and you decide. The lead hears first about any software a department wants, any renewal and any new AI tool. If the lead can only advise, every one of those decisions still lands on your desk.

**A calendar.** A weekly working session, a standing slot in the monthly leadership meeting, a quarterly roadmap review with you, and regular days in the building, because you can't inventory a plant, or learn how the shipping macro works, over video.

**Money.** The contract rules out commissions, referral fees and resale margins from any vendor. If the lead's firm also builds software or runs training, and ours does, the agreement says so: any build the lead recommends gets its own quote, beside at least one option using something you already own, and you're free to hire someone else for it.

**The ending.** With one IT person, the risk is a system only the outsider understands. Agree how it ends while everyone gets along:

- every account, domain and license in the company's name, with admin logins you control and the lead's login shut off on the last day
- documentation of every system the lead touches, written as the work happens, kept where you own it
- a handover of 30 to 60 days, with a named person taking each duty
- company ownership of any code, scripts, prompts and configuration
- a short notice period after the first 90 days

Ask every candidate who took over from them at their last engagement. If they can't name the person they handed over to, keep looking.

Bring the lead in through the IT manager, not around them. If the IT manager reads this as a replacement and leaves in month two, what they know leaves with them. Have them sit in on the interviews, and put in writing what they'll own by month 12: the MSP meeting, the vendor register and the restore test. The controller keeps signing, now against the register and the limit. If the arrangement is designed so you can never do without it, you've bought dependency.

## Days 1 to 30: an inventory nobody has

In the first month the lead puts together:

- every system and subscription, who uses it and what it costs
- every contract, with its renewal date and notice period
- every admin account and whose name it's in: the domain registrar, the Microsoft 365 global admin, the ERP admin, the website host
- where data moves between systems, including by someone retyping it
- which AI tools people already use, and on whose accounts, down to the ChatGPT subscription on a customer service rep's card

That's the first month's output: the inventory, a name on every admin login, and every renewal for the next 18 months on one calendar.

The inventory has a second use. Only 20% to 30% of businesses listed for sale complete a transfer, the Michigan Economic Development Corporation said in a [September 2026 summary](https://www.michiganbusiness.org/news/2026/09/five-themes-from-medc-small-business-rfi/) of its small business request for information. Plante Moran's [May 2026 advice](https://www.plantemoran.com/explore-our-thinking/insight/2026/05/preparing-for-ma-in-construction-and-real-estate) to construction and real estate sellers warns that "informal or single-person IT models introduce key-person risk." A son or daughter taking over needs the same list, and the shipping macro is the first thing on it.

## By day 90: a roadmap the owner can explain

It should fit in the two minutes you'd give your banker, with the remaining decisions in order of date and dependency:

1. The MSP's written scope, before the short renewal runs out.
2. SharePoint and OneDrive permissions, before Copilot goes on for the office. Microsoft [says](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/microsoft-365/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) Copilot shows people only what they already have permission to see, so a payroll export in a folder everyone can open can turn up in anyone's answers.
3. The ERP decision through the spring, well ahead of end of support.
4. The dealer portal last, because it depends on the ERP.

By then you also have a vendor and contract register: the inventory, kept current, with an owner on every line. Expect it to turn up a first saving, usually overlap: two file-sharing services, or licenses still billing for people who left.

## Months 4 to 12: the big decisions get made

Read the end-of-support letter for what it actually ends: usually new features and full support, not the software. Epicor's final on-premises Kinetic release, for one, is tentatively set for January 2028, with active support through 2029, [CIO reported](https://www.cio.com/article/4114170/epicor-sets-timeline-to-sunset-on-prem-erp-as-cloud-becomes-the-only-path-forward.html) in January 2026. Ask your vendor in writing what continues past its date: tax updates, security fixes, and support for the database and server underneath, which have dates of their own. [SQL Server 2016](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/products/sql-server-2016)'s extended support ended July 14, 2026, and [Windows Server 2016](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/products/windows-server-2016)'s ends January 12, 2027.

The vendor will frame the decision as upgrade or replace. Start instead from what the system has to do in three years: take dealer orders without anyone retyping them, configure a chair with dozens of fabric and finish options without a spreadsheet on the side, and show dealers lead times they believe. Weigh every option against that list, including two more years on what you have.

Write down what the shipping macro does, then move it into the ERP or rebuild it as a small tool someone besides its author can support. Moving it into the ERP needs no build at all, so that option sits beside any quote to rebuild it, ours included. [Build, buy or configure](/insights/build-buy-or-configure/) covers the general decision.

## Month 12: continue, shrink or hire

The checks start at month six. By then you should see:

- You can explain the roadmap without notes.
- At least one contract has been renegotiated on terms you wrote.
- Nobody has learned about a renewal from an invoice.
- The IT manager runs more than they did at the start.

Don't wait for month 12 to give notice if there's no inventory by day 30, a recommendation lands on the lead's own firm with nothing beside it, the IT manager is in fewer meetings rather than more, or hours creep up with no decision attached.

At month 12, check that the big decisions are made (or put off on purpose, with a date), the MSP's scope is rewritten and the register is in the IT manager's hands. Then decide year two in writing: continue if big decisions are still coming, shrink to a monthly session if the IT manager can run the rest, or hire full-time if the tally has passed two days a week, and have the fractional lead write the job description.

## Start with three logins and the decision table

This week, with nobody hired, check three logins. Start with the domain. Public WHOIS lookups often show a domain's holder as "redacted for privacy" under ICANN's [registration data rules](https://www.icann.org/en/contracted-parties/consensus-policies/registration-data-policy), so sign in to the registrar account and read the registrant contact. If nobody at the company can sign in, that's the finding, and a lapsed domain takes your email and website with it. If the registrant is a former employee or the old web developer, fix it while they still answer the phone. Under ICANN's [transfer policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/transfer-policy-2024-02-21-en) as of November 2026, a change needs their confirmation. In the Microsoft 365 admin center, see who holds the Global Administrator role: someone at the company should, never only the MSP. For the ERP, ask the vendor's support who is listed as administrator.

Then fill in the decision table and ask two things of each row: could the right answer be something other than renewing as is, and is it worth more than about $10,000 a year or able to stop shipping? If no row passes, renew and look again in six months. If only one does, say the ERP, buy a scoped project for it, such as an ERP selection, and hold off on a standing lead. If two or more pass and depend on each other, the way the portal depends on the ERP, and nobody neutral is deciding them, you've found the job.
