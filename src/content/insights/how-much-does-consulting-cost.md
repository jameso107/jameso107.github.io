The general manager of a 140-person farm equipment dealer group in Iowa has to put a number for outside AI help into the 2027 budget, and it's due this quarter. The group has five stores, each with sales, parts and service, and the GM wants help with two things: warranty claims that bounce back from the manufacturer, and parts that sit at one store while a customer waits at another. (The dealer group is invented.)

Three firms bid. A regional IT firm bills by the hour and estimates 150 to 250 of them. A consulting firm quotes a fixed fee for a four-week first step. A software vendor wants a 12-month platform subscription plus an implementation fee. The totals are several times apart, and none is obviously overpriced, because each is the price of a different job.

We couldn't find a trustworthy published source for consulting rates, so there's no hourly range here. Dealers who bought something similar are a better guide to price, and a 20 group (noncompeting dealers who share their numbers) is the place to ask, if you're in one.

## The same job, priced five ways

Consulting is sold in five shapes, and the shape decides who pays when the work runs long.

| Model | Who carries the risk | Fits when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly or daily | You, for every overrun | The scope is unknown and the job is short | No cap and no list of deliverables |
| Fixed project | The consultant, for overruns | You can write down what "done" means | Assumptions that turn into change orders |
| Monthly retainer | You, for hours that go unused | A tool is live, or you want standing advice | Rollover rules and long notice periods |
| Fractional role | You, if the seat has no authority | Senior judgment, a day or two a week | No decision rights in writing |
| Value-based | The consultant, if the metric is sound | You already have a baseline both sides trust | A number either side can game |

Hourly billing (a day rate is the same thing in bigger pieces) leaves every overrun with you. Buyers anchor on the rate, but the hours decide the bill: a firm that charges 20% less and takes half again as long costs 20% more (0.8 x 1.5 = 1.2), and on an open scope nobody knows the hours. If you buy hours, get a not-to-exceed cap in writing and a call when half of it is spent.

A fixed fee moves the overrun risk to the consultant, so it carries some cushion. The useful part is that both sides must write down what "done" means before anyone starts. Read the assumptions as closely as the price: one that proves false, such as a dealer management system (DMS) with no usable export, becomes a change order.

A retainer buys defined access, such as a block of hours or a standing call. Ask whether unused hours roll over and how much notice ends it. A 12-month commitment to advice you haven't tried yet is a bet.

A fractional role is a retainer with a seat attached, and our piece on the [fractional technology partner](/insights/fractional-technology-partner/) weighs it against a full-time hire.

Value-based pricing ties the fee to a measured result, often a share of savings. It needs a baseline you both trust and a number neither side can game, so it fits later, once a fixed project has settled [what to count](/insights/measuring-ai-roi/).

## What companies your size budget for AI

In the CFO Survey, run by the Richmond and Atlanta Feds with Duke University at the end of 2025, the median firm with fewer than 500 employees expected to spend $12,500 on AI in 2026 ([May 2026 working paper](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/research/national_economy/cfo_survey/academic_publications/AI_survey.pdf)), and nearly 60% of those firms planned less than $20,000 ([March 2026 summary](https://www.richmondfed.org/research/national_economy/cfo_survey/research_and_commentary/2026/20260325_research_commentary)). That median lumps together every firm under 500 people, so it tells a 140-person company little.

An Atlanta Fed survey of senior executives in March 2026 gives a figure that scales: more than half of firms expected to spend no more than $200 per employee on AI in 2026, counting software, subscriptions, hardware, worker training and IT staff support ([Atlanta Fed, May 2026](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/publications/policy-hub-macroblog/2026/05/06/how-much-firms-spending-on-ai-and-what-will-happen-to-headcounts)). At 140 people, $200 a head is $28,000 a year, and a first consulting engagement sits in that line beside the seats and the training.

So each bid has to answer one question before it's compared with the others: what will this change that a few seats and our own people couldn't?

## The bill that never appears on a proposal

Every proposal prices the consultant's hours. None prices yours. At the dealer group, three people will give about 120 hours to interviews, pulling past repair orders and returned claims, checking drafts and training: about 60 from the warranty administrator, who does most of the checking, and 30 each from the service and parts managers.

To price an hour, multiply the wage by about 1.43, the ratio of private employers' total cost per hour worked ($46.89) to wages ($32.82) in [BLS's June 2026 employer cost data](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm). It's a national average, so redo it with your own payroll and benefit costs. At an illustrative $30 an hour, $30 x 1.43 = $42.90, and 120 hours x $42.90 = about $5,150: nearly a fifth of that $28,000, on top of every invoice and before counting the GM's own hours.

A plan that puts testing in April or October, when service and parts are buried in planting or harvest, will slip, and on an hourly bid the slip is yours.

The rest of the bill sits outside the fee too:

- Seats and usage fees, which keep running after the consultant leaves. Ask for the monthly run cost at your volume, in writing.
- Data cleanup. Parts lookup across five stores works only if a part looks the same in each: the current number rather than a superseded one, bins recorded the same way. Proposals usually assume it's done.
- Integration. Connecting anything to your DMS may need the DMS vendor's cooperation, and some vendors charge for data access, on their own schedule. One phone call settles most of it (the questions are at the end).
- Management time: deciding that warranty narratives now start from a draft and making sure the parts counter uses the lookup. That's the GM and the service manager, for months.

Bain's [Technology Report 2026](https://www.bain.com/insights/the-ai-native-enterprise-absorption-is-the-new-advantage-technology-report-2026/) says the companies it works with have found that for every dollar spent on technology, four go to people and process. That's what Bain sees at large clients. At a dealer group's size we'd expect the direction to hold: a proposal that's all software is underpriced somewhere, and the difference lands on your people's calendars.

## Price the problem before judging the bids

Until someone prices the problem, no bid can be called cheap or expensive. Take 40 returned claims a month (an illustration; use your own count) at 30 minutes each to fix and resubmit: 20 hours x $42.90 = $858 a month, about $10,300 a year. Add the warranty labor and parts never recovered on denied claims (the claim reports have them), then the parts counters' hours a week spent hunting stock at other stores x the loaded rate x 50 weeks.

The total is the most a perfect fix could be worth in a year, and a first tool gets part of it. A bid whose first year costs more than the whole problem has answered itself.

## Three proposals, taken apart

### The hourly bid has no finish line

The IT firm proposes "discovery, recommendations and implementation as needed," billed monthly. There's no list of deliverables to check the invoices against, and nothing about what it assumes, such as whether the DMS can export repair orders. The top of the estimate is two-thirds more than the bottom, and that gap is your risk. Ownership isn't mentioned, so a half-built tool could end up in the firm's accounts. You can stop any month, but you stop with whatever exists.

### Four weeks, a fixed fee and a pass bar

The consulting firm will interview service writers, the warranty administrator and the parts counters at two stores, and map how a claim moves from a closed repair order to the manufacturer's portal. It will test drafting claim narratives (complaint, cause and correction) from 50 past repair orders against a pass bar written down first: no invented parts, labor or failure details, and every miss flagged. It ends with a written recommendation on both problems (buy, configure, build or skip) and prices the next step separately.

The bid assumes read access to two years of repair orders and returned claims, plus the 120 hours above, and puts integration and licenses out of scope in writing. The map, the test set and the results are yours, so you can hand them to the vendor or another firm and rebid step two. Stopping costs the fee and nothing more.

### A year of software, signed before any test

The vendor's bid is a 12-month term with a monthly fee per store, an implementation fee, and an "AI service assistant" for write-ups, warranty and parts. The fine print makes DMS integration "subject to DMS vendor cooperation" and assumes clean parts data. Your templates, prompts and history live in the vendor's platform, with no export terms, and stopping means paying out the year.

The platform may be the right buy if it already reads your DMS without a custom project, if two dealers on your DMS and brand will take your call and say whether the manufacturer accepts its narratives, and if the contract lets you out after a test at one store. Signing now buys a year before anyone has tested whether it can write an acceptable warranty narrative from your technicians' notes.

Our read: the fixed first step is the only bid that can be compared with an outcome. Discount that a little: it's the same shape as the audit we sell. The case for it rests on two terms you can ask of any bidder: a written finish line and a cheap way out. Ask the IT firm to restate its bid as a capped first step with a deliverable list, and the vendor for a six-week test at one store on past repair orders, with a way out if it misses the pass bar ([testing before you commit](/insights/prototype-before-you-commit/) covers the setup). If the four weeks show the warranty problem is mostly a training problem, the next step may need no consultant at all.

## Eight questions that make the bids comparable

Send all three the same list and ask for answers in writing. These test the proposal; for vetting the people behind it, our piece on [what an AI consultant actually does](/insights/what-an-ai-consultant-does/) has its own list.

1. **Exactly what will you deliver?** A list of things you can hold, not a paragraph about approach.
2. **Who does the work, and for how many hours?** Ask for the hours and roles behind every price, fixed fees included. If a bidder won't say what a fixed fee assumes, there's nothing to check the number against.
3. **What are you assuming about us?** Data access, staff hours, the DMS vendor's cooperation. Ask which of them, if wrong, changes the price.
4. **What's out of scope?** Get the list in writing, so nobody argues about it in month three.
5. **What will it cost to run after delivery?** The support rate, any usage cap and the overage charge. From the vendor, also how far the per-store fee can rise at renewal, and the fee if you add or close a store.
6. **Who owns the code, prompts, data and accounts, and how do we get them back?** They should be yours, in a format you can export. If not, the price is understated.
7. **How will success be measured, and by whom?** If nobody can answer, you're paying for effort.
8. **What does it cost to stop after each step?** Ask for it in dollars for each step, and for what you keep.

Then put the answers on one footing:

| Line it up | Hourly | Fixed first step | Platform |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash out in 2027 | Top of estimate x rate | The fee | Implementation + 12 x 5 store fees |
| Your people's hours | Not stated: ask | About 120 | Not stated: ask |
| Running cost after | Not stated | Priced in step two | The monthly fee |
| Cost to stop after step one | Hours billed so far | The fee | Rest of the year |
| What you know at the end | Whatever exists | Pass or fail on 50 claims | Whether it works, after paying |

Price the hourly bid at the top of its estimate. A bid that leaves a row blank has told you what it left out. For the 2027 budget, carry three lines instead of one: the first step's fee, your own people's hours, and a step-two line you release only if the first step meets its pass bar.

## Free and state-funded help to use first

Before paying anyone, get a free opinion. [Iowa's SBDC](https://iowasbdc.org/) offers no-cost counseling at 15 regional centers, and the SBDCs in [Michigan](https://michigansbdc.org/), [Indiana](https://www.isbdc.org/) and [Wisconsin](https://wisconsinsbdc.org/) advise at no cost too; bring them the bids and the scope paragraph below. Manufacturers can also call their state's [Manufacturing Extension Partnership center](https://www.nist.gov/mep/about-nist-mep): federal money pays half of each center's costs and client fees help cover the rest, so ask what yours charges.

State training money pays for job-specific training only. Ohio's [TechCred guidelines](https://development.ohio.gov/business/workforce-development/tech-cred/techcred-program-guidelines/program-guidelines) list subscriptions, software platforms and user licenses as ineligible, and Michigan's Going PRO pays for training toward a credential; its [FY2026 guide](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/-/media/Project/Websites/leo/Documents/WD/Industry_Businesses/Talent-Fund/Employer-Guides/FY26-Going-PRO-Talent-Fund-Employer-Guide.pdf) ruled out "AI literacy training showcasing generic uses of common AI tools." Iowa's [260F job training program](https://workforce.iowa.gov/grow/260f) runs through the community colleges and excludes retail businesses, so ask yours whether a dealership counts.

## Write the scope before anyone sends a price

Write four sentences: the job, the number you want to change, what "done" looks like, and what you'll provide. For the dealer group:

*We want warranty claim narratives drafted from technicians' repair order notes at all five stores. Today [how many] claims a month come back from the manufacturer for missing information, and we want that cut in half within six months of going live. Done means the warranty administrator approves drafts for every store and we've counted returned claims for 60 days. We'll provide read access to two years of closed repair orders and returned claims, and 120 hours from three named people.*

Before the 2027 budget goes in:

- Have the warranty administrator count last year's returned and denied claims, and the dollars never recovered. The count goes in the brackets.
- Call your DMS vendor: can you export closed repair orders and parts inventory by store, and what does an integration cost and take? Give every bidder the answer.
- Ask the manufacturer's dealer support contact whether it already offers a tool for claim narratives or finding parts, and what your dealer agreement lets you send to an outside platform.
- List the people the project needs, their hours, and the months they can't give them.
- Book a free SBDC session and bring the paragraph.

Then send the paragraph and the eight questions to every bidder. One who can't price against them is telling you how the project would go.
